The Pomodoro Technique: how 25 minutes of focus beats a whole afternoon of drift

Francesco Cirillo built the Pomodoro Technique in the late 1980s and named it after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he actually used to time his own work. The mechanics are almost embarrassingly simple: pick one task, set a timer for 25 minutes, work until it rings, then take a five-minute break. After four rounds, take a longer one. That is the entire method.

What makes it work is not the 25 minutes. It is the commitment device. A task you have been avoiding — the draft, the migration, the tax form — is enormously easier to start when you know you are only committing to 25 minutes of it. Most people do not procrastinate because a task takes four hours. They procrastinate because a task feels unbounded, and unbounded work is terrifying.

Why 25 minutes, specifically

Attention research consistently puts the span of uninterrupted focus somewhere between 20 and 45 minutes for most adults, and it gets shorter with fatigue and longer with practice. Twenty-five sits comfortably in that band: long enough to reach genuine depth, short enough that you do not feel the first serious dip in attention setting in. Start there rather than the aspirational 90-minute block most people attempt and abandon by Tuesday.

The break is not optional. Five minutes away from the screen, ideally standing and moving, is what lets attention recover. The single most common way people break the technique is treating the break as a reward they have not yet earned, and then spending it back in the same tab they were just working in.

Running a full session

Block out four consecutive rounds before you start, because four is where the momentum breaks — this is the part almost everyone under-plans. Clear your desk. Close the tab that tempts you. Set your timer, start round one, and do not look at the countdown once it is running. Watching the clock is a form of anxiety, and it will cost you more than it gives.

After the fourth round, take fifteen to thirty minutes. Do not fill it with email — that is not a break, that is a lateral move into a different kind of work. When you come back, review what you finished, then either start another set or stop. Stopping on time is part of the discipline.

When to adjust the length

If you are consistently finishing rounds early and feeling restless, move to 30 or 35 minutes. If you are losing the thread and drifting at minute 18, drop to 20. The number is a starting point, not a doctrine. Track what you actually complete and tune from there.

Finally, measure sessions, not hours. A tracker that counts completed focus rounds is far more motivating than a list of hours worked, because hours worked rewards presence, and rounds completed rewards output. The timer on this site keeps a running count of finished sessions in the current tab for exactly that reason.

Common questions

How long should a Pomodoro break be?

Five minutes after each 25-minute round, and 15 to 30 minutes after every fourth round. The break should be away from the screen.

Is 25 minutes really enough to finish deep work?

For most tasks, yes. Complex tasks get several consecutive rounds. The point is that each round is a bounded unit you can actually start.

Should I use a Pomodoro timer for email?

No. Email is shallow work and fits better in a batched block. Reserve Pomodoro rounds for work that requires actual thought.
